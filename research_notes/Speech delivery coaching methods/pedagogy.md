# Evidence-based methods for teaching oral reading fluency and prosodic delivery (guided vs independent practice)

Context: Cadence (the user reads a fixed passage and is compared with several good human readings) wants a guided "Practice mode" (pacer/highlight plus model readings) and an unguided "Test mode" (scored). These notes collect what the research says and map it onto those two modes.

## 1. Fluency-building methods (repeated, assisted/model, echo, choral, NIM, reader's theatre) and effect sizes

### Takeaway
Repeated oral reading of the same passage, with a model and feedback, is the best-supported fluency method: NRP weighted ES = 0.41; Therrien (2004) ES = 0.75 for fluency and 0.73 for comprehension. Assisted approaches (reading with or after a model) do better than unassisted ones (Kuhn & Stahl 2003). That supports a Practice mode built on "listen to the model, read along, then repeat."

### Cited Findings
- **National Reading Panel (2000):** the meta-analysis of guided oral reading procedures found a weighted average effect size of **0.41** (moderate), with a "consistent and positive impact on word recognition, fluency, and comprehension" across grade levels — [NICHD NRP Report ch.3](https://www.nichd.nih.gov/sites/default/files/publications/pubs/nrp/Documents/ch3.pdf); summarized at [Reading Rockets](https://www.readingrockets.org/topics/fluency/articles/what-works-fluency-instruction)
- The NRP reviewed **77 studies** of Guided Repeated Oral Reading (GROR) with feedback. In GROR the reader orally rereads a single instructional-level text, usually **3–5 times**, with feedback from a teacher or a stronger reader — [Fluency NRP Updates (BRT, 2020)](https://brtprojects.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/Fluency-NRP-Updates-TRL-2020.pdf)
- **Therrien (2004), Remedial & Special Education:** 18 studies (1977–June 2001), ages 5–18, including students with learning disabilities. Mean ES = **0.75 for fluency** and **0.73 for comprehension** — [ASHA Evidence Map summary](https://apps.asha.org/EvidenceMaps/Articles/ArticleSummary/903ec865-92fd-4ca5-9cea-8534b161420a)
- **Kuhn & Stahl (2003):** "assisted approaches seem to be more effective than unassisted approaches." Repetitive approaches showed no clear advantage over nonrepetitive ones (reading more varied text). Effective fluency instruction goes beyond automatic word recognition to rhythm and expression — [Winthrop module summary](https://winthrop.edu/uploadedFiles/ceshs/edco/module/202-610-repeated-readings.pdf); [PMC2783645](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2783645)
- Two forms are defined. **Unassisted** repeated reading: the learner rereads until reaching a set fluency criterion. **Assisted** repeated reading: the learner rereads while or after listening to a teacher or a recording — [PMC2783645](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2783645)
- Kuhn and colleagues compared **FORI** (scaffolded repeated reading of one grade-level text per week) with **wide reading** (scaffolded reading of three texts per week). By year end both gave similar gains in word reading efficiency and comprehension, but wide reading showed benefits earlier, including in oral text reading fluency — [PMC2783645](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2783645)
- **Reader's theatre:** in a 10-week study, second graders gained **17 WCPM** while the control group gained about half that. Rasinski reports gains in word recognition, fluency and comprehension — [Free Spirit readers theater research report](https://cp.freespirit.com/estore/files/research/readers_theater_research_report.pdf)
- **Neurological Impress Method (NIM)** (the student and a fluent reader read aloud together, with the model slightly ahead): one study reports positive effects on rate, accuracy, prosody and comprehension across reading levels — [TR Dizin study](https://search.trdizin.gov.tr/yayin/detay/254453). A study with remedial secondary students found **no significant comprehension difference** versus control — [CORE: NIM with remedial secondary students](https://core.ac.uk/works/9133064). Evidence is mixed and low quality.
- **Echo reading** (the model reads a phrase or sentence, then the learner repeats it): reported to improve fluency and comprehension (Mathes et al. are cited) — [TR Dizin echo reading study](https://search.trdizin.gov.tr/yayin/detay/142818). I found no meta-analytic effect size.
- **Reading Plus Guided Window** (a computer pacer that reveals and hides text in a window moving left to right at a rate calibrated to the student): with struggling third graders, the guided silent reading group had significantly higher scores, an effect of about **1 SD** — [Reading Plus efficacy: guided silent reading](https://www.readingplus.com/efficacy/exploring-a-guided-silent-reading-intervention/); [USU record](https://digitalcommons.usu.edu/teal_facpub/1881); [Rasinski et al. 2014](https://textproject.org/wp-content/uploads/papers/Rasinski-Samuels-Hiebert-Petscher-Feller-2014-The-relationship-between-a-silent-reading.pdf). Caveat: this research is published or hosted by the vendor, and it covers *silent* reading.

### Inferences
- In Practice mode, sequence the support as: listen to the model, then read along or echo with the model, then repeat alone. This matches the assisted repeated reading evidence. 3–5 repetitions per passage is the evidence-based dose (NRP GROR).
- A moving highlight/pacer is the Guided Window idea applied to oral reading. Its evidence comes from vendor-linked silent-reading studies, so treat the pacer as a scaffold, not the core of training.
- The FORI/wide-reading result suggests a passage library can beat endless repetition of one passage. Cadence's fixed-passage design should rotate passages once a criterion is reached.

### Gaps
- I did not extract moderator results from Therrien (2004), such as model reading vs none, corrective feedback, criterion-based vs fixed-number rereading, or transfer vs non-transfer fluency ES. The full text was not accessible, so the commonly quoted figures are not verified here.
- I found no reliable meta-analytic effect sizes for choral reading, echo reading or NIM on their own.
- Almost all of this evidence is from children (K–12). I found no adult public-speaking studies of repeated reading.

## 2. Paced reading and Read Naturally's cold vs hot timing (maps to Test vs Practice)

### Takeaway
Read Naturally's cycle has the same structure Cadence wants. Step 1: an unpracticed one-minute **cold timing** sets the baseline. Step 2: the student reads along with an audio model (usually 3 times). Step 3: the student practises alone (3–10 times) until reaching a goal. Step 4: a **hot timing** checks against the goal, and both scores are graphed side by side.

### Cited Findings
- The Read Naturally Strategy has three components:
  - **Teacher modeling:** the student "reads along quietly with a recording of the story, typically three times."
  - **Repeated reading:** the student practises "without audio support three to ten times until able to read it accurately, with expression, and at the goal rate."
  - **Progress monitoring:** a challenging goal, plus immediate feedback; "students can see before-and-after feedback."
  - Source: [Read Naturally Strategy](https://readnaturally.com/research/read-naturally-strategy); [Read Live User Guide: the strategy](https://userguide.readnaturally.com/readlive/the-read-naturally-strategy.html)
- **Cold timing:** the student reads the story for the first time, unpracticed, for one minute. It sets a baseline so the student can see improvement after practice. Read Live calculates WCPM and graphs it — [Read Naturally knowledge base: one-minute timing](https://readnaturally.com/knowledgebase/q-and-a/40); [Read Live user guide, hot timing](https://www.readnaturally.com/read-live-user-guide/part-two-read-naturally-live/implementing-the-steps/completing-the-pass-step/conducting-a-hot-timing)
- **Hot timing (the "Pass" step):** happens when the student finishes a story. The teacher listens, counts errors, and **rates the student's expression**. The teacher enters the words missed and the expression rating — [Read Naturally knowledge base](https://readnaturally.com/knowledgebase/q-and-a/40)
- The norms table (below) says students "scoring 10 or more words below the 50th percentile using the average score of two unpracticed readings from grade-level materials need a fluency-building program." Screening uses *cold* (unpracticed) readings — [Hasbrouck & Tindal 2017 table, Read Naturally](https://www.kirtlandschools.org/Downloads/2017%20Oral%20Reading%20Fluency%20Norms.pdf)

### Inferences
- Map the cycle onto Cadence as follows:
  - **Test mode = cold timing:** first unaided read, scored as the baseline.
  - **Practice mode = read-along, then independent practice:** pacer and model readings.
  - **Test mode again = hot timing:** unaided, scored, shown next to the cold score.
- Show cold vs hot as a before/after pair; this is Read Naturally's main motivational device.
- Keep cold reads unpracticed. The norm-referenced comparison only makes sense for unpracticed text, so hot-read gains on a practised passage should not be compared with norms.

- I could not retrieve Read Naturally's exact pass criteria (how the goal is set relative to the cold score, number of errors allowed, expression scale points). The pages did not render. These need a manual check before being quoted.

## 3. Rate norms and prosody rubrics (NAEP, MDFS, acoustic prosody measures)

### Takeaway
Rate norms level off around grade 5–6: the 50th percentile is about 146 WCPM in spring. Above that point, rate alone stops telling readers apart, and prosody rubrics take over. Two rubrics are standard: NAEP's 4-level scale and the Rasinski–Zutell MDFS, which scores 4 dimensions from 1–4 for a total of 4–16. In acoustic terms, matching an adult model's pitch contour and placing pauses correctly predict reading skill (Schwanenflugel lab). Comparing a reader's contour with adult model readings is the same thing Cadence does.

### Cited Findings
- **Hasbrouck & Tindal (2017)** WCPM norms, compiled from DIBELS, DIBELS Next and easyCBM — [Reading Rockets 2017 update](https://www.readingrockets.org/article/fluency-norms-chart-2017-update); [Read Naturally PDF](https://www.kirtlandschools.org/Downloads/2017%20Oral%20Reading%20Fluency%20Norms.pdf); report at ERIC ED594994

  50th percentile WCPM:

  | Grade | Fall | Winter | Spring |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | 1 | – | 29 | 60 |
  | 2 | 50 | 84 | 100 |
  | 3 | 83 | 97 | 112 |
  | 4 | 94 | 120 | 133 |
  | 5 | 121 | 133 | 146 |
  | 6 | 132 | 145 | 146 |

  Grade 6 spring percentiles: 90th = 204, 75th = 173, 25th = 122, 10th = 91. Average weekly growth in grade 6 is only 0.3 WCPM, so rate has plateaued.
- **NAEP 2002 Oral Reading Study (grade 4):**
  - Levels 3–4 are "fluent" and levels 1–2 "non-fluent"; 61% were judged fluent — [EdWeek](https://www.edweek.org/leadership/more-focus-on-reading-fluency-needed-study-suggests/2005/11); [NCES 2006469](https://nces.ed.gov/nationsreportcard/pubs/studies/2006469.aspx)
  - The level descriptors:
    - Level 1: "word by word"
    - Level 2: "local grouping"
    - Level 3: expresses phrases and clauses, with more than half the words read with appropriate expression
    - Level 4: "expresses sentence structure and meaning," with more than three-quarters of the words read with appropriate expression
  - Average WCPM was 120. The correlation between WCPM and the NAEP reading score was **0.75**.
  - Source: [NCES ORF results](https://nces.ed.gov/nationsreportcard/studies/orf/results.aspx)
- **Rasinski & Zutell Multidimensional Fluency Scale (MDFS):** four dimensions (**expression & volume, phrasing, smoothness, pace**), each scored 1–4, for a total of 4–16. A total below 8 suggests a concern. By year end, students reading grade-level text should score at least 9 (the top half on each dimension) — [Scholastic MDFS PDF](https://www.scholastic.com/content/dam/PostPurchase/Megabook_of_Fluency/Downloads/EARS%20Multidimensional%20Fluency%20Scale.pdf); [Kent State Ohio Literacy Alliance](https://literacy.kent.edu/ohioliteracyalliance/fluency/admin.pdf); [Cox Campus: Measures of Prosody](https://files.coxcampus.org/Measures-of-Prosody.pdf)
- **Acoustic prosody (Schwanenflugel lab, e.g. Miller & Schwanenflugel 2008):**
  - Measures used: number of **pause intrusions** (pauses inside sentences, not at punctuation), **intersentential pause duration**, **phrase-final comma pause duration**, **child–adult pitch match** (correlating the child's F0 contour with adult readings of the same text), and **sentence-final pitch change**.
  - Pause intrusions, comma pause duration and child–adult pitch match predicted reading accuracy and comprehension.
  - Fewer pauses came before adult-like pitch contours developed. Pitch contour predicted later fluency even after word reading speed and accuracy were taken into account.
  - Sample: 92 children, measured at the end of grades 1 and 2, with outcomes in grade 3.
  - Source: [PMC2805245](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2805245); [Miller dissertation, UGA](https://openscholar.uga.edu/record/18004/files/miller_justin_200808_phd.pdf); [Benjamin MA thesis, UGA](https://openscholar.uga.edu/record/20092/files/benjamin_rebekah_a_200908_ma.pdf)

### Inferences
- Cadence's reference-comparison approach (multiple good human readings) has direct precedent in the "child–adult pitch match" measure: correlation of the learner's F0 contour with an average of adult model contours. Pause intrusions and comma/sentence pause durations are well-validated, easy-to-explain features for feedback.
- MDFS's four dimensions (expression/volume, phrasing, smoothness, pace) are a natural, research-backed vocabulary for Test-mode score breakdowns; NAEP's 4 levels offer a coarse overall band.
- Adult rate targets cannot be read off the child norms. The grade 6 spring 50th percentile (146 WCPM), with the 75th percentile at 173, only shows where reading rate levels off.

- I did not verify Benjamin et al. (2013), "Comprehensive Automatic Rating Scale," or other automated prosody scoring validity numbers.
- I did not verify any adult oral-reading WPM norms.

## 4. Scaffolding fading, guidance hypothesis, feedback timing, testing effect

### Takeaway
The guidance hypothesis says constant concurrent feedback creates dependence and hurts unaided retention. A recent pre-registered meta-analysis found only a small, non-significant benefit from reducing feedback frequency (g = 0.21 at delayed retention). This favours fading the guidance and scoring in an unguided test mode, but the effect is modest and should not be overstated.

### Cited Findings
- The **guidance hypothesis** comes from Salmoni et al. (1984). Feedback on every trial is thought to make learners rely on augmented feedback rather than their own internal feedback, which hurts retention — [Frontiers in Neuroscience 2016](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnins.2016.00251/full); [PMC4893479](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4893479)
- Terminal feedback appears more effective than concurrent feedback for novices' retention. Some studies contradict this and find that concurrent auditory feedback on performance can *improve* retention. One study found a reduced frequency of **67%** better than 100% or lower frequencies — [Frontiers in Neuroscience 2016](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnins.2016.00251/full) (search summary; mixed results)
- **McKay, Hussien, Vinh, Mir-Orefice, Brooks & Ste-Marie (2021)**, pre-registered meta-analysis: 37 studies, N = 1,145. Reduced vs 100% feedback frequency gave **g = 0.21 at delayed retention** and **g = 0.06 at immediate retention**. The timepoint difference was not significant (p = .47), and "the present data fail to support timepoint-dependent feedback frequency effects" — [Journal of Expertise / SCAPPS](https://www.scapps.org/jems/index.php/1/article/view/2529)

### Inferences
- Practice mode can use concurrent guidance (pacer, live highlight, model audio), but it should fade. Example: full pacer, then pacer only at phrase boundaries, then no pacer, with feedback given after the read rather than during it. Learning should be judged by Test mode (unguided) scores, not by Practice-mode performance.
- Because the meta-analytic effect is small, fading is a sensible default rather than a proven large win. A/B testing it inside Cadence would be worthwhile.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve primary sources for "desirable difficulties" (Bjork) or the testing/retrieval-practice effect (e.g. Roediger & Karpicke 2006) in this pass. They are well known but not cited here, so the report writer should not attach numbers without verifying.
- I found no studies of fading a visual pacer specifically for oral reading.

## 5. Shadowing and visual pitch-contour feedback in L2 prosody training

### Takeaway
Shadowing (repeating a model's speech in near-simultaneous imitation) improved L2 comprehensibility and fluency but not accentedness (Foote & McDonough 2017). Audio-visual pitch displays beat auditory-only feedback for learning intonation in several studies (de Bot 1983; Hardison 2004), though not all studies agree.

### Cited Findings
- **Foote & McDonough (2017), Journal of Second Language Pronunciation:**
  - Design: 16 ESL learners shadowed short dialogues on iPods for **8 weeks**, at least **4 times/week for 10+ minutes**.
  - Outcomes: they improved significantly on all speaking measures (comprehensibility and fluency in extemporaneous speech, and imitation of the model) **except accentedness**, and were largely positive about the activity.
  - Source: [CiNii record / search summary](https://cir.nii.ac.jp/crid/1363670318626960768); [JALT TLT article](https://jalt-publications.org/sites/default/files/pdf-article/45.6tlt-art3.pdf)
- **Hamada (2016):** lower-intermediate learners made significant listening gains from shadowing sessions of 10–15 minutes, 3–4 times a week, over 6 weeks — [Migaku summary (secondary, low quality)](https://migaku.com/blog/language-fun/shadowing-for-language-learners-a-2026-practical-guide). Verify against the primary source.
- **Visual pitch feedback:**
  - de Bot & Mailfert (1982): a 45-minute intonation-perception training session significantly improved English intonation production.
  - de Bot (1983): **audio-visual feedback was more effective than auditory feedback alone** for learning intonation.
  - Some other studies found no effect.
  - Hardison (2004) gave native English learners of French 3 weeks of training with a real-time computer pitch display. Benefits generalized to novel sentences and to segmental accuracy.
  - Source: [Language Learning & Technology, Jan 2004 (Hardison)](https://doaj.org/article/581055d4d4ec401da83bbc04117ec8c9); [CALICO: contextualized computer-based L2 prosody training](https://journals.equinoxpub.com/CALICO/article/view/23182)
- **Olson & Offerman, "Maximizing the effect of visual feedback for pronunciation instruction"** (Journal of Second Language Pronunciation) concerns how to design visual feedback for pronunciation — [Benjamins](https://www.benjamins.com/catalog/jslp.20005.ols)

### Inferences
- Shadowing maps directly onto a Practice-mode "read with the model" sub-mode, like NIM and choral reading. Its strongest evidence is for fluency and comprehensibility, not for native-like accent. This fits a delivery coach, which is about flow and expressiveness.
- An overlaid pitch contour (the learner's against the reference band) is supported as a Practice-mode aid. Show it after each attempt rather than continuously, in line with section 4.

### Gaps
- I found no effect-size meta-analysis for visual pitch feedback. "Better Accent Tutor" and WinPitch outcomes were not verified.
- I did not access the Hamada (2016) primary source.

## 6. Public-speaking pedagogy: deliberate practice, evaluation, and pause placement

### Takeaway
Listeners rated speech as most natural with pauses of about 0.6 s, or with sentence pauses about twice as long as comma pauses (0.3/0.6 s or 0.6/1.2 s). Longer pauses make speech sound slower even when articulation rate is unchanged.

### Cited Findings
- **Liu, Nakajima, Chen, Arndt, Kakizoe, Elliott & Remijn (2022), Frontiers in Psychology:**
  - Listeners: native English, Chinese and Japanese.
  - With equal pauses at commas and periods, **0.6 s** got the highest naturalness ratings.
  - With comma and period pauses set separately, naturalness was best at 0.6/0.6 s or at a **1:2 comma:period ratio** (0.3/0.6 s or 0.6/1.2 s), which matched or beat the original speech.
  - "Perceived speech rate decreased as the physical pause duration increased."
  - Source: [PMC8874014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8874014)
- Filled pauses (especially non-initial ones), speech rate and mean F0 add up in shaping perceived speaker certainty. False starts and the number of disfluencies lower perceived competence. Longer pauses lowered likability and effectiveness ratings, though this may depend on where the pause falls — [Frontiers in Psychology 2022 epub](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.778018/epub); [Niebuhr, Interspeech 2019](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2019/niebuhr19c_interspeech.html)

### Inferences
- Pause scoring in Cadence can use the reference readings as the norm, with a sanity band near 0.3–0.6 s at commas and 0.6–1.2 s at sentence ends. Pauses inside phrases ("intrusions," section 3) should be penalised separately from pause length at punctuation.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed evaluation of the Toastmasters evaluation protocol in this pass.
- I did not verify primary deliberate-practice sources (Ericsson) as applied to speaking.
- A popular claim that speakers who pause several times a minute score higher for "authenticity" (from analysis of more than one million talk ratings) came only from a marketing blog ([Maptive](https://www.maptive.com/pause-during-presentations-ways-to-master-it)). It is unverified and should not be cited.
