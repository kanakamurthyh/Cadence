# Indian speech sources for consensus-envelope datasets

The only source delivering six or more identical-text Indian-accented English readings tonight is the **Speech Accent Archive**, which holds 172+ South Asian speakers reading the same 69-word "Please call Stella" passage under CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 — a licence that allows derivatives but binds them to non-commercial share-alike. For a richer multi-reader pool without accent confirmation, LibriVox's Tagore and Naidu weekly poetry projects supply 14–17 public-domain readers of single 30–90 s texts, though none of those readers is verified as Indian. Historical recordings from Nehru, Gandhi and Bose are individually almost certainly public domain in India, but every known copy is a single unique performance, so they contribute a famous-speaker anchor rather than a consensus envelope. The practical path for a clean Indian-accent dataset is to combine Speech Accent Archive (primary identical-text pool), AccentDB (eight Indian-English speakers in a soundproof booth, CC BY-NC 4.0), and — if a clean derivative licence matters — newly commissioned CC0 readings of the Preamble to the Indian Constitution, whose 85-word English text is effectively a Government work and is read at mass events across the country.

## LibriVox Indian-author poetry provides 14–17 renditions but unconfirmed accents

Three LibriVox Weekly Poetry projects give the most renditions of a single short Indian-authored text in the public domain. **Tagore's "XVIII" from *Fruit-Gathering*** ("No: it is not yours to open buds into blossoms") is the best fit: 16 readers, durations ranging from 58 to 89 seconds, all within the 30–90 s target ([LibriVox project](https://librivox.org/xviii-by-rabindranath-tagore/); [Internet Archive item `xviii_2202.poem_librivox`](https://archive.org/details/xviii_2202.poem_librivox)). The text comes from Project Gutenberg #6522 and the files follow the URL pattern `https://archive.org/download/xviii_2202.poem_librivox/xviii_tagore_<initials>_64kb.mp3`. **Sarojini Naidu's "The Gift of India"** has 17 readers at 91–131 s, one of whom — Shantanu Santra (reader 16124) at 91 s — has an Indian name, making it the only weekly project with even a single possibly-Indian reader ([LibriVox project](https://librivox.org/the-gift-of-india-by-sarojini-naidu/); [IA item `the_gift_of_india_2104.poem_librivox`](https://archive.org/details/the_gift_of_india_2104.poem_librivox)). At 91–131 s, most readings exceed the 90 s cap; the shortest (Santra's and Caitlin Buckley's 91 s) just pass. **Tagore's "The First Jasmines"** adds 14 readers at 79–116 s, mostly within range, with no Indian-named readers ([LibriVox project](https://librivox.org/the-first-jasmines-by-rabindranath-tagore/); [IA](https://archive.org/details/firstjasmines_1002_librivox)). LibriVox's licence is unambiguous — **"LibriVox recordings are Public Domain in the USA"** — so redistribution and modification are unrestricted, and no NC or SA condition applies ([LibriVox reader page](https://librivox.org/reader/3588)).

The accent situation is weaker than the reader counts suggest. LibriVox's "Celebration of Dialects and Accents" volumes covering 35 and 20 self-labelled readers contain no Indian or South Asian entry, confirming that Indian volunteers rarely self-identify ([IA item `dialect_accent_0909_librivox`](https://archive.org/details/dialect_accent_0909_librivox)). Across the three weekly projects, Indian-named readers number exactly one (Shantanu Santra). A second tier exists: **RajVO** (reader 12236) has read roughly 100 weekly poetry texts under an Indian-name, and wherever RajVO appears the text already has 20+ other readers, giving a ready-made consensus pool with one potentially Indian-accented voice ([LibriVox reader 12236](https://librivox.org/reader/12236)). Aparna Vishwanatham, Sowmya and Leena Manohar each provide a reading of "Where the Mind Is Without Fear" (Gitanjali 35), which has only four standalone SPC readings but three Indian-named ones — at 60–72 s, squarely in range ([SPC 090](https://librivox.org/short-poetry-collection-090-by-various/); [SPC 094](https://librivox.org/short-poetry-094/); [SPC 274](https://librivox.org/short-poetry-collection-274-by-various/)). No accent has been verified by listening; the name inference could be wrong in both directions. The recommended first step before building any envelope from LibriVox is a 60 s listening sample from each of the ten Indian-named candidates.

India-themed Laurence Hope ("Garden of Kama") weekly projects add 12–22 readers of texts in the 49–89 s range. **"Among the Rice Fields"** has 22 readers (56–89 s) including two Indian-named readers (Asha and Edmond Aggabao) ([LibriVox project](https://librivox.org/among-the-rice-fields-by-laurence-hope/); [IA](https://archive.org/details/among_the_rice_fields_2008.poem_librivox)); **"Unanswered"** has 19 readers (71–112 s) including AfsanaA; **"The Hut"** has 12 readers (49–77 s) including Garfield Dsouza and RajVO. These are British colonial texts voiced in Indian settings, which affects naturalness but not the technical identical-text requirement. All are public domain in the USA.

## Historical recordings are individually PD but unsuitable for multi-rendition work

**Gandhi's 1931 "Spiritual Message to the World"** is the most legally clean historical recording. Two Wikimedia Commons copies exist at about 364 s each, both tagged with `{{PD-India}}`, `{{PD-1996}}`, and a UK sound-recording rationale ([Commons file M. K. Gandhi's spiritual message.oga](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:M._K._Gandhi%27s_spiritual_message.oga); [Commons file Gandhi — His Spiritual Message… 1931.mp3](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Gandhi_-_His_Spiritual_Message_to_the_World,_17_October_1931.mp3)). The reasoning is sound: a 1931 recording's 60-year term under India's Copyright Act s.27 ended around 1991, and being in the public domain in India on 1 January 1996 blocked US URAA restoration, so the `{{PD-1996}}` tag is correct ([OSM wiki GODL summary](https://wiki.openstreetmap.org/wiki/Open_Geospatial_Data_from_Government_of_India)). A timed Commons transcript exists. The audio is noisy gramophone-disc quality, but the licence is the most firmly established of any Indian historical speech recording.

**Nehru's "Tryst with Destiny"** is more complicated. The Internet Archive holds copies at 4:40 and 5:29, both shorter than the ~8-minute speech, suggesting they are excerpts or edits rather than the full live recording ([archive.org `HindSwaraj-Speech-03-1`](https://archive.org/details/HindSwaraj-Speech-03-1)). The Public.Resource.Org uploader asserts CC0, but this is an uploader's claim, not a grant from Prasar Bharati or All India Radio; treat it as "probably PD in India", not as a verified CC0. The audio quality is 1947 broadcast. Critically, Nehru died on 27 May 1964, so the literary work (the text) became public domain in India on 1 January 2025 under the life + 60 rule — meaning **new readings of the text are now unencumbered in India**. The Wikimedia Commons copy is tagged `{{PD-old-70}}`, which is technically the wrong rationale, adding residual uncertainty for US redistribution under the Music Modernization Act ([Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Tryst_with_Destiny-_Speech_by_Pt._Jawaharlal_Nehru.ogg)).

**Bose's 1943 Tokyo radio speeches** reach 11–18 minutes per file under `{{PD-Japan}}`, sourced from YouTube with weak provenance ([Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Speech_of_Netaji_Subhas_Chandra_Bose_from_Tokyo_Radio,_1943.ogg)). No Ambedkar, Vivekananda or Naidu speech recordings exist in open archives: the BBC 1955 Ambedkar interview is BBC copyright; the circulating "Vivekananda 1893" clips are confirmed fakes by Belur Math; Naidu and Tagore's surviving discs are privately held ([Belur Math](https://media.belurmath.org/clarification-on-swami-vivekanandas-voice-recording-swami-suvirananda-video-6719/)).

The multi-rendition problem is the decisive constraint. There is exactly one openly licensed English reading of the **Preamble to the Indian Constitution**: a 49.8 s Malayalam-language recording under CC0 ([Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:%E0%B4%AD%E0%B4%B0%E0%B4%A3%E0%B4%98%E0%B4%9F%E0%B4%A8%E0%B4%AF%E0%B5%81%E0%B4%9F%E0%B5%86_%E0%B4%86%E0%B4%AE%E0%B5%81%E0%B4%96%E0%B4%82_-_Preamble_to_the_Constitution_of_India_Read_in_Malayalam.ogg)). The Internet Archive search returned no English Preamble recordings and no "Tryst with Destiny" readings by anyone other than Nehru. **For any historical text, the multi-reader consensus pool must be newly commissioned.** PIB and Sansad TV audio are explicitly not usable: PIB's policy requires recordings be reproduced "accurately and not in a misleading context", which is incompatible with PSOLA flaw injection ([PIB Copyright Policy](https://pib.gov.in/Content/102_2_Copyright-Policy.aspx)).

## Speech Accent Archive is the only large identical-text Indian-English pool

The **Speech Accent Archive** (George Mason University) is the single source that trivially meets the ≥6-identical-text requirement for Indian-accented English. It holds readings of one fixed 69-word elicitation paragraph — "Please call Stella. Ask her to bring these things with her from the store: Six spoons of fresh snow peas, five thick slabs of blue cheese…" — by **approximately 197 South Asian speakers**, distributed across Hindi (34), Urdu (29), Bengali (24), Nepali (18), Gujarati (17), Telugu (15), Tamil (13), Punjabi (12), Marathi (11), Kannada (9), Malayalam (8), Konkani (4), Oriya (2) and Sindhi (1) ([Hindi list](https://accent.gmu.edu/language/hindi/); [Tamil list](https://accent.gmu.edu/language/tamil/)). Every recording is paired with metadata (sex, city, country of birth, age of English acquisition) and a ground-truth transcript is fixed in the design, so no transcription work is needed. Audio is fetched at `https://accent.gmu.edu/audio/<lang><n>.mp3` and measured durations from six Indian-language samples range from 26.3 to 35.2 s at 44.1 kHz mono 128 kbps MP3 ([hindi1 sample](https://accent.gmu.edu/samples/hindi1/)).

The licence is **CC BY-NC-SA 4.0**, quoted verbatim on the site as "BY-NC-SA 4.0 license from Creative Commons" with copyright attributed to Steven H. Weinberger and Matthew C. Kelley, George Mason University ([GMU How-to](http://accent.gmu.edu/howto/)). The NC condition means commercial redistribution of derived audio (including PSOLA-modified flaw injections) is prohibited; the SA condition means all published derivatives must carry the same licence. For a non-commercial hackathon entry, these conditions are workable, but they must be disclosed. Derivative datasets — including the ladder clips for the consensus envelope — cannot be published under a more permissive licence.

The 26–35 s clip duration is a structural constraint. The elicitation paragraph is only 69 words, and even a slow speaker finishes in under 40 s. This sits at the low end of the 30–90 s target and many clips will fall below 30 s after silence trimming. For the project's wav2vec2 forced aligner, the Stella paragraph poses no accent problems and the text is entirely transcribed, but the short duration limits how many flaw regions can be meaningfully injected into a single clip. A practical workaround is to treat the passage as a full delivery item rather than trying to build a 60–90 s excerpt. Grouping by L1 gives at minimum 8 languages with ≥6 speakers, and the Hindi group alone (34 speakers, filtered by India birthplace) can support a 12+ reading consensus envelope on a single passage.

## AccentDB and IndicTTS add clean booth audio at small scale

**AccentDB** is the second-best identical-text source for Indian-accented English and the best on audio quality. Eight speakers across four L1s (Bangla 2, Malayalam 3, Odiya 1, Telugu 2) read at least 25 Harvard sentence sets in a professionally designed soundproof booth with an Audio Technica AT2005USB and Tascam DR-05, with each set repeated three times, yielding **8 speakers × 75+ sentence sets × 3 takes** of identical text ([AccentDB paper arXiv:2005.07973](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2005.07973); [accentdb.org](https://accentdb.org/)). The licence is **CC BY-NC 4.0** (non-commercial, derivatives allowed, no share-alike), which is less restrictive than Speech Accent Archive in one respect: derivatives need not be released under the same terms. The core release is 2.8 GB (6,587 files). Each Harvard sentence is 2–3 s; concatenating one set of 10 sentences yields a ~25–30 s passage. For a consensus envelope, the small speaker count (8 total, 3 per L1) limits the envelope width but the clean audio and repeated-take structure are ideal for flaw injection baseline.

**IndicTTS** (IIT Madras) is potentially the highest-quality source, with studio-quality WAV and CC BY 4.0, giving approximately 26 Indian-English voices across 13 language groups (typically one male and one female speaker per language who also record English). The AIKosh mirror confirms "Attribution 4.0 International (CC BY-4.0)" — the most permissive licence of any source reviewed here ([AIKosh Hindi female mono](https://aikosh.indiaai.gov.in/home/datasets/details/hindi_female_mono_indictts_phase3.html)). The critical unresolved question is whether the English sentence set is identical across all language groups: if it is, IndicTTS-English delivers about 26 speakers per shared sentence, making it the only fully-open (CC BY 4.0) identical-text option. The IITM database page at `https://www.iitm.ac.in/donlab/indictts/database` was unreachable during research and must be verified manually before building from it.

**Common Voice v25.0** English holds 152,535 clips from 3,011 India-and-South-Asia-accent speakers under CC0 ([Mozilla Data Collective CV 25.0](https://prod.datacollective.mozillafoundation.org/datasets/cmndapwry02jnmh07dyo46mot)), but because the corpus spans ~1.72 million distinct sentences, the expected count of Indian-accent speakers per sentence is approximately one. This rules out Common Voice for identical-text grouping except for the earliest pre-2019 sentences, where duplicates were acknowledged as common. Verifying this requires downloading the validated TSV and groupby on sentence filtered to Indian-accent `client_id`s.

**Svarah** (AI4Bharat, CC BY 4.0, 117 speakers, 9.6 h) is explicitly excluded: the data-collection design "ensured that no two participants got the same sentence" ([Svarah paper arXiv:2305.15760](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2305.15760); [HF card](https://huggingface.co/datasets/ai4bharat/Svarah)). **Kathbath/IndicSUPERB** likewise states "a given sentence is only spoken by one speaker and never repeated" ([arXiv:2208.11761](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2208.11761)). Neither is usable for the consensus envelope design.

## Ranked plan for building two new texts tonight

The following rankings apply to building 1–2 new texts for Cadence's Indian-speaker component as of the evening of 2026-10-14.

**Text option 1 — Speech Accent Archive "Please call Stella" (recommended first, build tonight).** Download all India-birthplace Hindi samples from `https://accent.gmu.edu/audio/hindi<n>.mp3` (confirmed by birthplace metadata, e.g., `hindi1` = Nagpur). Filter to clean SNR and durations ≥27 s. Pick the top 6–12 by aligner confidence to form the consensus envelope. Licence: CC BY-NC-SA 4.0. Transcript: the fixed Stella paragraph, freely available on the GMU How-to page. Accent: confirmed Indian English, L1 documented per speaker. Clips from other L1 groups (Bengali, Gujarati, Telugu, Tamil) can add 6+ renditions each with no additional download steps. The wav2vec2 aligner handles the Stella paragraph reliably. Build time: ~1 h to download, filter and align a Hindi group. **Caveat:** flaw-injected ladder clips must be distributed under CC BY-NC-SA 4.0; mark this in `docs/DATA_LICENSING.md`.

**Text option 2 — LibriVox Tagore "XVIII" (build alongside, public-domain, unrestricted licence).** Download all 16 files from IA item `xviii_2202.poem_librivox` at the pattern `https://archive.org/download/xviii_2202.poem_librivox/xviii_tagore_<initials>_64kb.mp3` (initials: ks, lah, alp, pr, lcw, bk, tg, el0, kz, mpm, gg, aps, kla, dl, pjw, ls). Durations: 58–89 s, all in range. Transcription: Project Gutenberg #6522. Licence: Public Domain USA, no restrictions. Accent: none confirmed as Indian; run the wav2vec2 aligner's confidence score as a proxy for clear diction; manually listen to RajVO's file from the "Among the Rice Fields" project (the closest overlap) to calibrate expectations. This text adds a second text with 16 readers and zero licence conditions — the strongest addition to the existing T1–T6 roster in terms of multi-reader count. Label it T7-Indian-PD and leave accent confirmation as pending until a listening check is done.

**Deferred option — Commission new CC0 Preamble readings (best long-term, not achievable tonight).** The English Preamble (85 words, ~30–40 s reading time) is effectively a public-domain Government work, widely memorized, and licensed under no restriction for reproduction under s.52(1)(q) of the Indian Copyright Act. A Lingua Libre upload campaign or a targeted CC0 recording drive with 6–10 Indian readers would yield the cleanest possible corpus: known accent, identical text, no NC/SA restrictions, Cadence-specific consent forms. This is the highest-priority action after the hackathon deadline.

**Do not use:** Svarah, Kathbath, IndicVoices, Common Voice without per-sentence groupby verification, PIB/Sansad TV/Mann Ki Baat audio, the Ambedkar BBC recording, or any "original Vivekananda voice" clip.

## Conclusion

The research confirms a structural gap: no existing openly licensed repository supplies six or more Indian-accented readings of the same 30–90 s English passage without either a short-duration constraint (Speech Accent Archive at 26–35 s) or an unconfirmed-accent caveat (LibriVox public-domain poetry). For tonight's build, combining the Speech Accent Archive's Hindi group as an Indian-English accent text with LibriVox Tagore "XVIII" as an unrestricted multi-reader text covers both requirements at low engineering cost. The IndicTTS-English identical-sentence question, once resolved by checking the IITM database page, could supersede both by providing studio-quality CC BY 4.0 audio with confirmed Indian accents. The deepest long-term investment — commissioning CC0 readings of the Preamble — is also the simplest conceptually, requiring only coordination with willing readers and a Lingua Libre or local recording session, and it would produce the only identical-text Indian-English corpus with no licence conditions whatsoever.
